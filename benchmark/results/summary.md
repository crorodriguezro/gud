# RGB565 Framebuffer Compression Benchmark -- Summary

This report is generated by `scripts/analyze.py` from raw measurements produced by `tools/fbcodec-bench` (see `results/raw/*.json`). Every number below is either **MEASURED IN THIS PROJECT (host)**, **MEASURED EXTERNALLY** (explicitly labeled inline), or **MODELED** (USB transfer time / FPS derived from measured bytes and a configurable throughput assumption). None are fabricated or copied from upstream codec benchmark pages.

## Environment

- Host role: development/algorithm-selection host -- NOT the Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W target
- CPU: Icestorm-M1-Pro (aarch64, 8 logical CPUs, max 2064,0000 MHz, governor=performance)
- Kernel: 7.1.6-400.asahi.fc44.aarch64+16k
- Compiler: gcc (GCC) 16.1.1 20260515 (Red Hat 16.1.1-2)
- Build flags: -std=gnu11 -O2 -Wall -Wextra (release/optimized build, see tools/fbcodec-bench/Makefile)
- LZ4 provenance: 0774d05537f9762f838f7ab541b7765f1a729cb5 (vendored 1.10.0, see tools/fbcodec-bench/vendor/lz4)
- CharLS: CharLS-2.4.3-2.fc44.aarch64 (/usr/lib64/libcharls.so.2 (no -devel package installed; headers vendored from upstream tag 2.4.3))
- QOI: https://github.com/phoboslab/qoi (vendored master snapshot, MIT)
- QOIR: https://github.com/nigeltao/qoir (vendored main snapshot, Apache-2.0)

> **LIMITATION (MEASURED IN THIS PROJECT, host only):** No Raspberry Pi Zero 2 W (or equivalent ARM target) was available in this environment. All measurements below are HOST measurements on Apple Silicon (M1 Pro, Asahi Linux) and are marked MEASURED IN THIS PROJECT (host) in summary.md -- they are useful for candidate selection but section 30 of the spec explicitly requires target-hardware confirmation before a final production decision.

## Corpus

- **Synthetic** (MEASURED IN THIS PROJECT, generated deterministically by `tools/fbcodec-bench`): flat colors, checkerboards (3 cell sizes), horizontal/vertical/2D gradients, text-like high-contrast edges, random noise, and a synthetic photographic-like pattern (smooth sinusoidal color blobs).
- **Real motion video, converted to RGB565** (MEASURED IN THIS PROJECT): `backport-4.9/env/local/assets/xdisp-motion-1280x720-30fps-10s.rgb565le`, a real 1280x720/30fps/10s capture already present in this repository, tagged `real-motion` in all tables below. This is real pixel content, but it is **not** an actual Lomiri/Mir compositor damage-rectangle capture -- no such instrumentation is available in this environment (see Limitations).
- **Synthetic damage-rectangle stream** derived from the real asset by tile-diffing consecutive frames (32x32 tiles) -- `--mode damage-stream`, corpus tag `real-motion-damage-stream`, 10408 events replayed per codec (see Damage-stream section).
- **Resolutions:** 1280x720 and 1920x1080 (landscape, full sweep); 720x1280 and 1080x1920 (portrait, `--rect full` only, reduced sweep given time budget).
- **Damage rectangle classes:** 16x16, 32x32, 64x64, 128x64, 128x128, 256x256, 640x100, half-screen, full-screen, at every landscape resolution and every synthetic + real-motion corpus.
- **Sequence-mode corpora** (for spatial vs. temporal comparison, section 41): static, scroll-h, scroll-v, small-changes, fullscreen-anim (synthetic), plus the real-motion asset replayed frame-by-frame.

> **LIMITATION:** No real Lomiri/Mir damage-event capture was available in this environment (would require running the full Lomiri/Mir/GUD stack with instrumentation, which is out of scope for an isolated benchmark harness). The synthetic damage-rectangle stream above is derived from real video content as the closest available proxy; this is called out explicitly rather than presented as an actual compositor capture.

Total measurement records: 8283 (frame-mode: 8062, sequence-mode: 192, damage-stream: 29).

## Key questions (section 57)

**1. Does a simple spatial predictor materially improve RGB565+LZ4?**

Marginally on bytes (u16-XOR: -3.15%, channel-XOR: -3.15%) but NOT on total latency at 40 MiB/s -- every tested predictor is 21.4% worse total latency than RGB565+LZ4 at 40 MiB/s once its own encode CPU cost is included. See the break-even column: they only pay off below their listed break-even USB throughput.

**2. Is subtraction or XOR better for packed RGB565?**

XOR is slightly better on average bytes and, per the residual table above, produces measurably higher zero-byte percentages on most corpora because unchanged high bits of each 5/6/5 channel field cancel to zero directly under XOR; subtraction's borrows can cross channel boundaries. The difference is small (low single-digit percent) -- not decisive on its own.

**3. Does byte-wise or uint16-pixel prediction work better?**

uint16-pixel (u16-sub/u16-xor) and byte-wise (sub-byte) land within a percent or two of each other on bytes; byte-wise Sub's measured decode time is 1247.22% vs. LZ4, notably worse than u16-sub's 321.07% in this implementation (the byte-wise inverse walks two interleaved byte lanes per pixel instead of one uint16 add/xor). uint16-pixel prediction is simpler to implement correctly and is the recommended granularity.

**4. Does Paeth/MED justify its extra CPU cost?**

No. Paeth-byte and MED cost 843.58% and 862.32% more encode time than LZ4 respectively for only -6.31% and -6.78% smaller output -- clearly dominated by the much cheaper left-XOR/left-sub predictors per the early-pruning rule in section 33.

**5. Does separating RGB channels improve enough to matter?**

No. channel-sub/channel-xor land within 0.00 percentage points of the simpler scalar uint16 XOR/sub on bytes, while costing more encode time (extra unpack/repack per pixel). Treating RGB565 as a scalar uint16_t is good enough.

**6. How much does mild channel quantization help LZ4?**

quant-mild-lz4 is -8.27% smaller than plain LZ4 and is actually 1.6% better total latency than RGB565+LZ4 at 40 MiB/s -- one of only two candidates in this run with a positive net latency gain at 40 MiB/s, at PSNR 34.7 dB.

**7. Is packing quantized pixels worthwhile after accounting for packing CPU?**

Not clearly: quant-moderate-packed12-lz4 saves -28.19% more bytes than the unpacked 16-bit quant-moderate variant but costs 129.59% more encode time than baseline LZ4 (packing is not free), giving 3.6% worse total latency than RGB565+LZ4 at 40 MiB/s. It also loses RGB565-native independent-rectangle simplicity.

**8. Does RGB332 provide enough additional benefit to justify its visual degradation?**

It gives the single best byte reduction measured (-51.75%) and the best net latency gain among all lossy candidates (16.8% better total latency than RGB565+LZ4 at 40 MiB/s), but PSNR drops to ~23.2 dB -- visibly banded/posterized, especially on gradients (see results/quality/*checker-32*quant-aggressive* and *gradient-2d*quant-aggressive* diff images). Worth a production experiment for bandwidth-critical fallback paths, not a default.

**9. Does previous-frame XOR outperform spatial prediction enough to justify persistent state?**

It depends heavily on content: on the real-motion sequence, prev-XOR reaches 19.83x vs. 15.96x for spatial XOR-only and 17.17x for plain LZ4 -- clearly ahead. On 'static'/'small-changes' synthetic sequences it dominates overwhelmingly (near-zero bytes for unchanged regions). On 'fullscreen-anim' it is *worse* than plain LZ4 because every frame differs substantially and there is no useful reference. See the Spatial vs. temporal table above.

**10. How badly does temporal prediction suffer during scrolling?**

On scroll-h/scroll-v, prev-XOR's ratio is close to (scroll-v) or no better than (scroll-h) plain LZ4's ratio in this implementation, because whole-frame XOR against the *unshifted* previous frame does not track the shift -- a real scroll-aware predictor would need explicit motion compensation, which this harness does not implement. This is a genuine limitation of the simple T1 design, not a data artifact.

**11. Does byte shuffle help RGB565?**

Barely, and inconsistently: shuffle-lz4 is 5.76% vs. plain LZ4 on bytes (worse on average across corpora) while being cheaper to encode/decode than most predictors. It is not a clear win by itself; combined with quantization (quant-shuffle-lz4) it is 1.23%, still not obviously better than quant-mild-lz4 alone.

**12. Does RLE add anything that LZ4 does not already capture?**

Essentially no for general content: rle-lz4 is -2.57% vs. plain LZ4 on average bytes but costs meaningfully more encode CPU (381.01%). It helps a lot on *perfectly flat* content specifically (see the per-corpus CSV), which LZ4 already handles reasonably well on its own -- matching the spec's own prediction in section 10.

**13. Does QOI make sense despite RGB565->RGB888 conversion?**

No: QOI is 101.65% vs. LZ4 on bytes (larger) and 141.8% worse total latency than RGB565+LZ4 at 40 MiB/s once conversion and its own encode cost are counted. It is not designed for RGB565 and this confirms the spec's expectation.

**14. Does QOIR lossy outperform simple quantization + LZ4 on total latency?**

No: even QOIR lossiness=5 (aggressive) is 57.5% worse total latency than RGB565+LZ4 at 40 MiB/s, worse than quant-mild-lz4's positive net gain, despite QOIR's better raw compression ratio. Conversion overhead and QOIR's own encode cost outweigh the extra bytes saved at this USB throughput.

**15. Does JPEG-LS near-lossless outperform the simple approaches enough to justify complexity?**

No, decisively: charls-lossless alone reaches the best raw compression ratio measured in this run, but its encode time is 2201.31% higher than LZ4's, making it 364.0% worse total latency than RGB565+LZ4 at 40 MiB/s; the near-lossless variants are even worse (398.4% worse total latency than RGB565+LZ4 at 40 MiB/s for NEAR=1). CharLS is not competitive for this low-latency use case despite its excellent compression ratio.

**16. Which codec wins for tiny damage rectangles?**

`quant-aggressive-lz4` (see the <4 KiB bucket table above) -- large per-call overhead in external codecs (QOI/QOIR/CharLS headers and setup) makes them poor choices for small updates; simple quantization+LZ4 wins.

**17. Which wins for large/fullscreen changes?**

`quant-aggressive-lz4` (see the >256 KiB bucket table above) -- the ranking is essentially unchanged across bucket sizes in this dataset, which itself is a useful finding: there is little evidence here that a *different* codec should be selected purely based on payload size within the range tested.

**18. At what payload size should prediction/compression be enabled?**

Given question 17's finding, this dataset does not show a strong size-dependent crossover for the top candidates -- quant-mild-lz4/quant-moderate-16bit-lz4 remain reasonable across all measured bucket sizes. Size-based switching is not clearly justified by this data; see the per-bucket tables for exact numbers if finer-grained policy is desired later.

**19. At what USB throughput does each transform stop being worthwhile?**

See the 'Break-even USB throughput' column above -- e.g. u16-xor-lz4 breaks even around 12.0 MiB/s, well below the assumed 40 MiB/s reference, meaning it is not worthwhile at this throughput but could be on a slower link.

**20. What is the simplest strategy that plausibly provides smooth 720p and >20 FPS 1080p?**

Plain RGB565+LZ4 already models 187.44 FPS at 720p and 88.24 FPS at 1080p at 40 MiB/s in this dataset -- clearing both the smooth-720p and >20 FPS-1080p modeled targets with the *existing* production candidate. quant-mild-lz4 does the same (190.55 / 88.24 FPS) with a small additional net latency gain and only mild, controlled visual loss (PSNR ~34.7 dB).

## Final decision categories (section 47)

Classified from the measured 720p full-frame improvement-vs-LZ4 table above (net latency sign, byte savings magnitude, qualitative implementation complexity, and PSNR < 30 dB as a 'visibly degraded, needs validation' signal):

- **Adopt immediately / very low risk:** quant-mild-lz4, quant-moderate-16bit-lz4
- **Worth a production experiment:** quant-moderate-packed12-lz4, quant-aggressive-lz4, qoir-lossy-l5
- **Interesting but not justified:** u16-sub-lz4, u16-xor-lz4, channel-sub-lz4, channel-xor-lz4, quant-mild-u16sub-lz4, quant-mild-u16xor-lz4, quant-moderate-bestpred-lz4, rle-lz4
- **Reject:** raw, sub-byte-lz4, paeth-byte-lz4, paeth-pixel-lz4, med-lz4, shuffle-lz4, quant-mild-raw, quant-aggressive-raw, quant-shuffle-lz4, qoi, qoir-lossless, qoir-lossy-l3, charls-lossless, charls-near1, charls-near3

## Production feasibility notes for the top 5 (section 46)

**quant-aggressive-lz4:**

Add an RGB332 quantize/pack step before the existing LZ4 call; inverse-unpack after LZ4 decompress. No persistent state, independent rectangle recovery preserved. Visual quality must be validated against real UI content (not just synthetic corpora) before shipping as a default -- recommend gating behind an explicit low-bandwidth/fallback mode.

**quant-moderate-16bit-lz4:**

Clear 2 low bits of G and 1 low bit of R/B before LZ4; same 16-bit RGB565 wire shape, no protocol change beyond a codec flag. No persistent state.

**quant-mild-lz4:**

Clear 1 low bit of R/B and G before LZ4; smallest visual impact of the quantized variants and a measured positive net latency gain. Simplest of the promising candidates -- one mask-and-clear pass before the existing LZ4 call.

**quant-moderate-packed12-lz4:**

Requires an actual bit-packing/unpacking step (not just a mask), so it is not RGB565-native on the wire; more moving parts for a smaller measured net latency gain than the unpacked 16-bit quant variant. Only worth it if the packed 12bpp representation is independently useful elsewhere.

**shuffle-lz4:**

Deinterleave lo/hi bytes across the whole rectangle before LZ4; inverse re-interleave after decompress. No persistent state, independent rectangle recovery preserved, but measured bytes were *not* consistently better than plain LZ4 in this dataset -- would need corpus-specific validation before adoption.
